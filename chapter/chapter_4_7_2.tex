\section{Introduction}

This chapter develops a working model of the emotional states that shape how I think, act, and make decisions. Rather than treating emotions as passive experiences, they are understood as \textbf{operational modes} that influence performance, direction, and sustainability over time.

Two questions guide the structure:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Which emotional states are most relevant for how I want to operate?
	\item Which states are underrepresented, yet necessary to sustain the others?
\end{enumerate}

The key idea is that these states are not independent. They form a \textbf{dynamic system} with transitions, dependencies, and functional roles:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textit{Flow} enables construction and execution,
	\item \textit{Serenity} provides emotional grounding,
	\item \textit{Boredom} allows reset and re-sensitization,
	\item \textit{Perturbation} drives structural change.
\end{itemize}

Understanding this system makes it possible to deliberately move between states, instead of passively reacting to them. The goal is not to remain in a single state, but to \textbf{operate within the right transitions at the right time}.

The chapter is therefore structured in two parts: first, the emotional states themselves and their interactions; second, their translation into concrete environments and decisions (see Section~\ref{PR_PHSI}), where the model becomes operational. This translates the emotional model into concrete environments and decisions. The focus shifts from internal states to \textbf{where and how these states can be activated and sustained}.

The structure follows two layers:
\begin{itemize}
	\item First, \textbf{Payed Hierarchies with Social Impact}, environments in which the model operates most effectively (e.g., paid hierarchies with social impact), including how roles, people, and constraints shape flow, learning, and contribution.
	\item Second, \textbf{study and research} as a parallel domain, functioning as continuous, lower-intensity perturbation and long-term exploration.
\end{itemize}

Together, these contexts define how the system is not only understood, but \textbf{deliberately embedded into real-world decisions}.

\section{Emotional Ramification}

\subsection{Flow}

\paragraph{History – From Single Challenge to Flow}

The Flow State represents the domain of \textbf{deep immersion, creative intensity, and self-forgetting engagement}. It replaces the former category of \textit{Challenge}—not because challenge disappears, but because it is \textit{contained within} flow.\\

Previously, the structure assumed that one should identify a single challenge and focus on achieving it. This created a discrete, goal-bound mode of operation.\\

The shift reframes the objective: the desired outcome of a given day is to work in a state of flow such that a \textbf{meaningful finish can be achieved}. Flow is therefore not the goal itself, but the mode of execution that enables completion.\\

Long- and short-term goals (challenges) still matter. However, they are primarily \textit{means to an end}. The emphasis moves away from abstract, verbal constructs (e.g., “a challenge”) toward a continuous experiential target: \textit{how I want to operate most of the time}.

\paragraph{General Conditions — Play, Experimentation, Construction}

Flow emerges when three elements converge:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item A clearly defined yet open-ended direction.
	\item Sufficient time and cognitive space for deep thinking.
	\item The ability to test, iterate, and observe consequences.
\end{enumerate}

Historically, I enter flow when I:
\begin{itemize}
	\item immerse myself in a new or stimulating environment,
	\item identify a meaningful problem or direction,
	\item design a solution (conceptual or technical),
	\item and test whether reality behaves as expected.
\end{itemize}

What matters is the loop:

\begin{center}
	Think $\rightarrow$ Construct $\rightarrow$ Test $\rightarrow$ Observe $\rightarrow$ Refine $\rightarrow$ Pause (boredom, serenity)
\end{center}

This loop transforms abstract thinking into embodied understanding. Flow emerges when I am simultaneously \textit{playing and building}.

\paragraph{Challenge as a Renewable Unit}

Challenges remain central, but they are no longer the primary organizing principle. The key shift is from a \textit{finite objective} to an \textit{ongoing mode of operation}.\\

Because flow has no natural endpoint, it requires a continuous renewal of challenges. A single, long-term challenge alone is not sufficient. Instead, challenges must be generated, engaged with, and completed at a rhythm that enables repeated entry into flow and \textbf{frequent closure}.\\

This leads to a simple operational principle:

\begin{center}
	\textit{Find a challenge, finish it, move to the next.}
\end{center}


The key constraint is not the number of challenges, but the \textit{conditions that allow challenges to emerge and evolve}.\\

Critical factors include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item the right \textbf{anchor person}, who defines a meaningful direction and creates a context in which success is satisfying,
	\item deliberately creating space for \textit{boredom} and \textit{serenity}, enabling recalibration and the discovery of new challenges.
\end{itemize}

The Flow State sits at the foundation of the Decision and Focus Space. When the right conditions are present, it requires minimal conscious effort to enter. However, sustaining it requires a natural oscillation:

\begin{center}
	in $\rightarrow$ out $\rightarrow$ re-entry
\end{center}

This alternation is not a failure of flow, but a necessary mechanism for its renewal.

\subsection{Serenity}

The emotional state \textit{serenity} derives from two very specifice objects:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textit{Tolle Familien haben.}
	\item \textit{Super Freunde haben.}
\end{itemize}

How I feel in those moments with family and friends in the most optimal way, I call \textit{serentiy}.

This state normally last not that long, a day or couple day and transformes into other states. Those emotional jollis might not last very long, but there are central as a general engine for the emotional state \textit{flow}. It provides they relevant emotional fundaiton. Speically the familiy part, provides a continuous sence of belonging and connection. This might not provide an continuous push for new, I receive through the flow state, but allows for a \textit{safe retriet}, form this energies can be startet.

\subsection{Being Bored — Regenerative State}

Boredom is not the absence of activity, but the \textbf{precondition for renewal}. Within this system, it functions as the state that enables the re-emergence of flow.\\

While flow represents active construction and serenity provides emotional grounding, boredom introduces \textbf{distance}. It removes immediate stimulation and interrupts continuous engagement. This creates a temporary state in which the system is no longer driven, but also not yet reoriented.

This gap has a functional effect: it \textbf{re-sensitizes} perception. Without boredom, challenges lose their appeal and flow becomes shallow. With sufficient boredom, even simple problems regain intensity and meaning.

\subsection{Pertubation}

 Perturbing my current environment is essential for counteracting the decay of value, especially in the flow-state environments I inhabit.
 
 For me, transitioning to new environments often requires a catalytic event rather than a slow, gradual shift. There is usually a barrier to overcome, and the natural gravitas of the flow state makes change difficult. Without a decisive disruption, adaptation tends to stagnate.

This connection is evaluated along three conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Belief:} I believe in the person — their abilities, character, or mission.
	\item \textbf{Learning:} I believe I can grow through exposure to them.
	\item \textbf{Contribution:} I believe I can meaningfully contribute to their vision, directly or indirectly.
\end{enumerate}

These three conditions collapse into two fundamental vectors that define the environment:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Upward vector:} I grow through exposure to someone I respect.
	\item \textbf{Outward vector:} I contribute value to their mission.
\end{itemize}

The mapping is not symmetric. The upward vector follows directly from the learning condition. The outward vector, however, depends on both contribution and belief: I can only sustainably contribute if I believe the person and their mission are worth supporting.

Flow emerges in the tension between these two vectors — between learning and contributing. If either component is missing, engagement decreases: \textit{If I cannot learn, stagnation appears.} $\bullet$ \textit{If I cannot contribute meaningfully, motivation fades.}


\paragraph{Conundrum Job Description}
The conclusion of this section is: I do not particularly prefer or rely on a fixed job description. 

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{My position} — where I am located within the company — provides hierarchical status and initial leverage. However, for most goals I am assigned or choose to pursue, I need to go beyond the leverage I formally possess.
	\item When people ask what my job is, the more accurate answer is: \textbf{I have goals aligned with my anchor person.} I apply my abilities (typically a subset of them, depending on the specific goal) to the goals I am assigned or choose.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Open Hypothesis — Individual vs.\ Collective Activation}

An open question remains whether the same emotional activation can emerge from a \emph{collective mandate} as from alignment with a single person. In other words: do I respond in the same way when a group entrusts me with a mission, compared to when I orient myself around a person I want to learn from?\\

The current hypothesis is that drive does not arise in a purely self-contained way. It appears to require a social point of reference: either a person or a group for whom something has to be achieved. Without such validation or external orientation, the activating force seems significantly weaker.\\


In that sense, the pure elective drive may be too weak on its own.\\

A stronger perturbation may arise only when responsibility for a group is present. One example was the collaboration with Christian. And feeling responsible for the group.

Study and research function as a form of \emph{continuous, low-intensity perturbation}. They maintain cognitive movement without requiring a full environmental shift.\\

\paragraph{Gradient from Applicable TO For its own sake}

This distinguishes study and research from \gls{p_PHSI}. While \gls{p_PHSI} is oriented toward usefulness, study and research are not.\\

Insights may later become useful—but this is a byproduct, not the objective. The work is done for its own sake. External validation is welcome, but optimizing for it in advance distorts the process.\\

Instead, a gradient emerges: topics may be immediately useful, useful later, transferable to other contexts, or simply meaningful without any direct application.

\paragraph{Dormant Idea: Group Mechanics as a Big Adventure}

There is an underlying idea developing: to build a model of the world grounded in intuitive understanding—primarily focused on human systems. This is not purely mathematical or mechanical; it is about structured human understanding.\\

Mathematics serves as a tool to increase precision and enable observation. The latest working title for this direction is \textit{group mechanics}.

\paragraph{Not Yet Science — Toward It}

At its current stage, this work is not yet science. It is a structured, but partially randomized, exploration of questions, patterns, and ideas.\\

The next step is to deliberately increase rigor: to move toward empirical, reproducible work that enables observation and experimentation. The goal is a transition from exploration to \textit{publication}—not necessarily limited to technical domains, but including broader human-centered fields.
